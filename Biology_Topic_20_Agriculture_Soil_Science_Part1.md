Q: The experiment demonstrates
A) soil attraction of water
B) soil porosity
C) soil capillarity
D) draining capacity soils
E) oil permeability
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Capillarity is the tendency of a liquid (here, water) to rise up a narrow tube or pore space (here, the soil spaces) against gravity — this is precisely what the experimental set-up, showing water rising through the soil columns, is designed to demonstrate.

Q: Which of these is not a type of soil?
A) Sand
B) Granite
C) Loam
D) Clay
E) Sandy loam
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Granite is a type of rock, not a soil — sand, loam, clay, and sandy loam are all genuine soil texture classifications (though granite can weather over time to eventually become part of laterite soil).

Q: The farmer cannot check soil erosion effectively by
A) Constructing his ridges for planting across the slope
B) cultivating cover crops
C) practicing strip cropping
D) clearing the land of vegetation
E) constructing terraces
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Clearing land of its vegetation cover actually exposes bare soil directly to the erosive forces of wind and water, making the soil more vulnerable to erosion — this is the opposite of an erosion-control measure. Ridging across slopes, cover crops, strip cropping, and terracing are all genuine, effective erosion-control practices.

Q: If three 30 cm lengths of glass tubing are tightly packed with clay, sand and loamy soils respectively and then stood in a beaker of water for one week, the level of water will be
A) lowest in the tube with clay
B) the same in all the tubes
C) lowest in the tube with loamy soil
D) highest in the tube with sandy soil
E) lowest in the tube with sandy soil
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: Sandy soil has the largest particle size and widest spaces between particles, giving it the least capillarity (weakest capillary rise) and lowest water retention of the three soil types, meaning water will rise the least (lowest level) in the sand-filled tube — clay, with its very fine particles, has the highest capillarity of the three.

Q: Leguminous plants, e.g. Mucuna, are usually planted in cultivated farmlands because they
A) enrich the soil with phosphates
B) provide animals with food
C) enrich the soil with organic nitrogen
D) protect the soil from being overheated
E) protect the microorganisms in the soil
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Leguminous plants host nitrogen-fixing bacteria within nodules on their roots, converting atmospheric nitrogen into a usable, organic form that enriches the soil's nitrogen content, benefiting subsequent crops.

Q: A few grams of dried soil were first heated until red hot and then further heated until no more smoke was released. This experiment was to determine the
A) amount of water in soil
B) percentage of water in soil
C) presence of humus in soil
D) resistance of laterites to heat
E) release of smoke from the soil
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Heating dried soil until red hot burns off its organic content (humus), which is released as visible smoke — the point at which smoke stops being released indicates that all the humus has been burnt away, allowing its presence/amount to be determined by this specific method.

Q: In which of the following plants is swollen shoot disease common?
A) Groundnut
B) Cocoa
C) corn
D) cotton
E) Rubber
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Swollen shoot disease (caused by a virus) is a well-known disease specifically and commonly affecting cocoa plants.

Q: Soil is said to be fertile if
A) it is black
B) it can supply usable nutrients to plant
C) The oxygen content is very high
D) artificial manure is added to it
E) it is not waterlogged
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Soil fertility is fundamentally defined by its capacity to supply the essential nutrients that plants need for healthy growth — soil colour, oxygen content, added manure, or waterlogging status are all related but secondary factors, not the core definition of fertility itself.

Q: Treatment of a poor soil with lime supplies
A) sulphur
B) calcium
C) phosphorus
D) sodium
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Lime (calcium-containing compounds such as calcium carbonate or calcium hydroxide) specifically supplies calcium to the soil, while also neutralizing soil acidity.

Q: If a farmer left his land uncultivated for five years before returning to it, this describes the practice of
A) modern agriculture
B) strip cropping
C) contour ridging
D) crop rotation
E) the bush fallow system
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: Deliberately leaving land uncultivated ("fallow") for an extended period to allow it to naturally recover fertility before returning to cultivate it is precisely the practice known as bush fallowing (shifting cultivation).

Q: A 28g soil sample was heated to a constant weight of 24g. When further heated to red hot and cooled, it weighed 18g. What is the percentage of humus in the soil?
A) 20.0
B) 55.6
C) 75.0
D) 25.9
E) 35.7
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: The initial heating to constant weight (24g) removes the soil's water content, leaving the dry soil mass; the further heating to red hot (removing organic humus by burning) reduces this to 18g. The humus content is therefore 24g − 18g = 6g, out of the original dry soil mass of 24g: (6/28) × 100% ≈ 21.4%, closest matching option A (20.0%) among those given, reflecting the standard percentage-of-humus calculation method.

Q: Erosion can be reduced along a slope by
A) ridging across slope
B) ridging up slope
C) ridging down slope
D) crop rotation
E) bush fallowing system
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Constructing ridges across (perpendicular to) the slope specifically interrupts and slows the downhill flow of water, reducing its erosive power — ridging up or down the slope, by contrast, would instead channel and accelerate water flow, worsening erosion.

Q: If a handful of soil is shaken with water and left to settle, the soil particles will settle from light to heavy particles as follows:
A) humus, clay, silt, sand and stones
B) humus, silt, clay, sand and stones
C) humus, sand, clay silt, stones sand
D) humus, sand, silt, clay, stones
E) clay, humus, silt, sand, stones
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: When shaken and left to settle by sedimentation, soil particles separate by density and size, with the lightest, smallest particles (humus, then clay) settling last (staying suspended longest, ending up in the uppermost layer), progressively followed by increasingly larger, heavier particles (silt, then sand, then the heaviest stones settling first at the bottom) — matching the settling order in option A.

Q: Leaching is
A) washing away of humus from the soil surface
B) reduction of soil aeration by pressure
C) soil erosion by means other than rainfall
D) loss of organic matter due to exposure to direct sunlight
E) washing out of chalk and limestone from upper layers of soil by heavy rains
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: Leaching is specifically defined as the downward washing away of soluble minerals (such as lime/limestone components) from the upper layers of soil to lower layers by percolating water (typically from heavy rainfall) — distinct from surface humus washing (a different, more surface-level erosion process) or other listed options.

Q: The process of soil erosion is usually from
A) rill, sheet, gully
B) gully, rill, sheet
C) sheet, gully, rill
D) sheet, rill, gully
E) gully, sheet, rill
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Soil erosion typically progresses through stages in this order: sheet erosion (uniform, even removal of a thin surface layer) → rill erosion (small, concentrated channels form as runoff increases) → gully erosion (rills widen and deepen further into larger gullies) — matching the sequence in option D.

Q: Soil erosion CANNOT be controlled by
A) planting cover crops
B) contouring of sloping ground
C) terracing of slopes
D) laying of much
E) burning of bush
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: Burning bush/vegetation actually removes the protective plant cover that would otherwise help hold soil in place, making the soil more vulnerable to erosion rather than controlling it — planting cover crops, contouring, terracing, and mulching ("laying of much") are all genuine, effective erosion-control practices.

Q: Water retention is highest in soils which are rich in
A) sand, poor in humus and devoid of clay
B) clay and sand, but poor in humus
C) clay and humus, but poor in sand
D) humus and devoid of sand
E) sand and humus, but poor in clay
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Both clay (due to its fine particles and high capillarity) and humus (due to its organic, water-absorbing properties) contribute strongly to water retention, so soil rich in clay and humus but relatively poor in the more porous, water-draining sand will have the highest overall water retention capacity.

Q: The origin of mineral particles in the soil is
A) humus
B) water
C) micro-organisms
D) weathered rock
E) organic matter
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The inorganic mineral particles that make up the bulk of soil originate specifically from the physical and chemical weathering (breakdown) of parent rock over time — humus, water, micro-organisms, and organic matter contribute to soil's organic and biological components, not its fundamental mineral particle origin.

Q: The initial volume of water poured into a bag of dry soil was 50ml and the amount that drained through was 35ml. The percentage water content of the fully soaked soil is therefore
A) 46.7
B) 25.0
C) 20.0
D) 30.0
E) 58.3
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The soil retained (50ml − 35ml) = 15ml of water out of the original 50ml poured in, giving a retained water percentage of (15/50) × 100% = 30%.

Q: The swollen shoot disease of cocoa tree is caused by a
A) virus
B) fungus
C) bacterium
D) protozoan
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: As established earlier, swollen shoot disease of cocoa is specifically caused by a virus (transmitted by mealybugs), distinct from other cocoa diseases like black pod disease, which is caused by a fungus.

Q: A large percentage of tropical soils tend to be acidic because they
A) contain large quantities of potash
B) contain large quantities of lime
C) lose a high proportion of their organic matter to running water
D) lose lime and potash from the top soil through rain action
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The heavy rainfall characteristic of tropical regions causes significant leaching, washing lime and potash (both alkaline, acidity-neutralizing minerals) out of the topsoil — this loss of neutralizing minerals is what leaves tropical soils tending toward acidity.

Q: The following are methods of soil conservation EXCEPT
A) contour terracing
B) strip cropping
C) contour ploughing
D) mixed grazing
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Mixed grazing (allowing livestock to graze freely, potentially overgrazing and compacting soil) is not a soil conservation method — contour terracing, strip cropping, and contour ploughing are all genuine, deliberate soil conservation practices.

Q: Samples of different soil types are packed in glass tubes whose lower ends are plugged with cotton wool. If these tubes are suspended in a trough of water, water will rise highest after a few hours in
A) sand
B) loam
C) clay
D) humus
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: As established earlier, clay's fine particles give it the highest capillarity among typical mineral soil types, causing water to rise highest through a clay-filled tube.

Q: The process by which lime is added to clay soils is known as
A) sedimentation
B) flocculation
C) leaching
D) manuring
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Flocculation is specifically the process where adding lime to clay soil causes the very fine clay particles to clump together into larger aggregates, improving the soil's structure, drainage, and workability.

Q: The mineral nutrient that is most bound to the soil is
A) phosphorus
B) calcium
C) iron
D) potassium
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Phosphorus tends to bind very tightly and become chemically "fixed" to soil particles (especially in the form of insoluble compounds like iron/aluminium phosphates), making it the mineral nutrient most strongly bound and least readily available to plants among the options.

Q: The mineral nutrient that easily gets leached out of the soil is
A) phosphorus
B) calcium
C) magnesium
D) nitrate
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Nitrate (a highly soluble form of nitrogen) is particularly prone to being washed (leached) out of the soil by percolating rainwater, unlike phosphorus, which tends to bind tightly to soil particles and resist leaching.

Q: Most commercial fertilizers are rich in salts of
A) Sodium, iron and calcium
B) nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium
C) iron, copper and nitrogen
D) calcium, sodium and phosphorus
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Commercial fertilizers are typically formulated to be rich in the three primary macronutrients most limiting to plant growth: nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium (the classic "NPK" fertilizer formula).

Q: 5g of oven dried soil was heated in a furnace for 24 hours, after cooling, it weighed 4.8g. What is the amount of humus in the soil?
A) 40.0g per 100g dry soil
B) 4.4g per 100g dry soil
C) 4.0g per 100g dry soil
D) 0.4g per 100g dry soil
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: The furnace heating burnt off the organic humus, reducing the soil mass from 5g to 4.8g — a loss of 0.2g of humus out of the original 5g sample: (0.2/5) × 100% = 4%, which scaled to a 100g dry soil basis gives 4.0g of humus per 100g dry soil.

Q: Most irrigated lands often become unproductive in later years because of
A) loss of fertility
B) increase in salinity
C) soil erosion
D) loss of water
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Continuous irrigation, especially in hot climates with high evaporation, often leads to a gradual build-up of dissolved salts in the soil (salinization) as water evaporates and leaves salts behind, eventually making the soil too saline for productive crop growth.

Q: Farmers practice crop rotation because it helps to
A) prevent soil erosion
B) allows two crops to be planted at the same time
C) helps to conserve soil fertility
D) is an alternative to shifting cultivation
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Crop rotation's primary agricultural benefit is helping to conserve and maintain soil fertility, by alternating crops with different nutrient demands (and incorporating nitrogen-fixing legumes periodically) to avoid depleting the soil of any one particular nutrient.
