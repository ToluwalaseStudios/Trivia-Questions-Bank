EXPLANATION: Position 2 in the diagram represents the upper epidermis, whose cells lack chloroplasts and are transparent, existing purely to protect the leaf and let light through — they take no direct part in photosynthesis itself, unlike the guard cells, mesophyll layers, and stoma, which are all directly involved in gas exchange or light-trapping.

A) 1

Q: Root hairs have their origin from
A) endodermis
B) pericycle
C) cortex
D) epidermis
E) xylem
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Root hairs are simply thin, tubular extensions of individual epidermal cells (specifically the outermost layer, also called the piliferous layer) of the root, formed to increase the surface area for water and mineral absorption.

Q: One significant difference between roots and stem is that
A) branch roots originate in the pericycle while branch stems do not
B) stems are always above the ground while roots are always below the ground
C) stems are positively geotropic while roots are negatively geotropic
D) stems are sometimes used for storage while roots are never so used
E) stems grow in both length and circumference while roots grow only in length
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Lateral (branch) roots arise internally from a specific tissue layer called the pericycle, deep within the root. Branch stems, by contrast, develop externally from axillary buds at the nodes, not from any internal pericycle-like tissue — this is a genuine structural distinction (the other listed statements are all factually incorrect: stems can be underground, roots can also be modified for storage, and both organs can thicken in girth).

Q: Which of the following tissues are made up of dead cells?
A) Meristematic
B) Xylem tissues
C) Cambium
D) Mesophyll
E) Palisade
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Mature xylem vessel elements and tracheids lose their living contents once fully developed, leaving behind hollow, lignified dead cell walls that form efficient water-conducting tubes. Meristematic and cambium tissues, by contrast, consist of actively dividing living cells, and mesophyll/palisade cells are living, photosynthetic cells.

Q: Root hairs are developed from the
A) root apex

D) endodermis

ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: As established earlier, root hairs are simple extensions of the outermost epidermal (piliferous) layer of a root, not from any of the internal vascular or meristematic tissues.

Q: In a dicot leaf, guard cells differ from other epidermal cells because they
A) have no definite shape
B) lack nuclei
C) are smaller
D) contain chloroplasts
E) lack vacuole
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Unlike ordinary epidermal cells, guard cells are unique in containing chloroplasts, which allow them to photosynthesize and produce sugars that drive the osmotic changes responsible for opening and closing the stomatal pore.

Q: Herbs differ from shrubs because they
A) do not produce fruits
B) are useful to herbalists
C) do not become woody
D) are only annuals
E) are only perennials
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Herbs have soft, green, non-woody stems and generally stay short, whereas shrubs develop woody stems that branch close to the ground. (Herbs can still be annual or perennial, and both herbs and shrubs may produce fruit, so "does not become woody" is the specific defining distinction.)

Q: Which of the following is NOT a characteristic of monocot plants?
A) occurrence of secondary thickening
B) parallel venation
C) scattered vascular bundles
D) floral parts arranged in threes
E) perianth is usually insignificant
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: True secondary thickening (growth in girth via a vascular cambium ring) is a feature of dicots (and gymnosperms), not typical monocots, which generally lack a continuous vascular cambium. Parallel leaf venation, scattered vascular bundles, floral parts in threes, and an often reduced/insignificant perianth are all genuinely characteristic of monocots.

Q: Which of the following statements is NOT true of the piliferous layer of a root? It
A) has a very thin cuticle
B) is the outermost layer of the cortex
C) may bear root hairs
D) breaks down as the root ages
E) is replaced by cork in old roots
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: The piliferous layer is actually the outermost layer of the epidermis of the root (where root hairs originate), not part of the cortex, which lies beneath the epidermis. It does have a thin cuticle (to allow water absorption while offering minimal protection), breaks down as the root matures, and is eventually replaced by protective cork tissue in older roots.

Q: Which of the following is seed bearing?
A) Mosses
B) Whistling pine
C) Algal filaments
D) Liverwort
E) Fern fronds
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: The "whistling pine" (a common name for a conifer such as Casuarina or Araucaria) is a gymnosperm and therefore produces true seeds. Mosses, liverworts, and ferns are all seedless plants that reproduce by spores, and algal filaments do not produce seeds either.

Q: In an angiosperm leaf, the xylem is
A) beside the phloem
B) surrounded by the phloem
C) above the phloem
D) around the phloem
E) in separate bundles from the phloem
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: In the vascular bundles of a typical angiosperm leaf, the xylem is positioned on the upper side of the vein, with the phloem lying below it — so the xylem sits "above" the phloem within each vascular bundle.

Q: Which of the following is common to a dicotyledonous stem and a monocotyledonous root?
A) Medullary rays
B) Central pith
C) Wide cortex
D) Narrow cortex
E) Pericyclic fibres
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Both a typical dicot stem and a typical monocot root are built around a distinct, wide central pith of parenchyma tissue, with vascular tissue arranged around this pith — a feature they share despite otherwise differing internal arrangements.

Q: Which of the following pairs are fully adapted to terrestrial life?
A) Ferns and algae
B) Ferns and mosses
C) Bryophyte and flowering plants
D) Flowering plants and conifers
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Flowering plants (angiosperms) and conifers (gymnosperms) both reproduce via pollen and seeds, entirely independent of external water for fertilization, making them fully adapted to a dry, terrestrial existence. Ferns, mosses, and algae, by contrast, all still require a film of water for their motile gametes to achieve fertilization, tying them to moist habitats.

Q: Which of the following has cones?
A) Angiosperm
B) Gymnosperm
C) Pteridophyte
D) Bryophyte
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Gymnosperms (such as conifers) are specifically characterized by bearing their naked seeds in cones, unlike angiosperms (which enclose seeds in fruit), pteridophytes (spore-bearing), or bryophytes (spore-bearing).

Q: The stem differs from the root in having the xylem and phloem strands
A) on the same radii
B) scattered
C) on alternate radii
D) towards the pith
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: In a typical dicot stem, the xylem and phloem are arranged together in the same vascular bundle, lying along the same radius (with phloem to the outside and xylem to the inside). In the root, by contrast, the xylem and phloem alternate around the central core rather than sitting on the same radius.

Q: The correct sequence of tissues in the anatomy of a young dicotyledonous stem, from the inside to the outside, is
A) pith, phloem, cambium, xylem, parenchyma, collenchyma and epidermis
B) xylem, phloem, cambium, cortex, endodermis, collenchyma and epidermis
C) pith, xylem, cambium, phloem, collenchyma, parenchyma and epidermis
D) phloem, xylem, cambium, cortex, endodermis, collenchyma and epidermis
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: From the very centre outward, a young dicot stem is arranged as: pith → xylem → cambium → phloem → (cortex, including collenchyma and parenchyma) → epidermis, which matches option C.

Q: Secondary thickening is initiated in a dicotyledonous stem by the
A) xylem parenchyma
B) secondary phloem
C) endodermis
D) cambium
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The vascular cambium is the meristematic (actively dividing) tissue responsible for producing new secondary xylem and secondary phloem, driving the increase in stem girth known as secondary thickening.

Q: Which of the following statements gives the BEST description of bark?
A) Tissues outside the vascular cambium
B) Cork-like tissues found only in stems
C) Brown tissues never found in primary growth
D) Cork cambium and cork tissues
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: "Bark" broadly refers to all the tissues lying outside the vascular cambium in a woody stem (including secondary phloem, cork cambium, and cork) — this is the most technically accurate, general description among the options.

Q: In the transverse section of the leaf of maize, vascular bundles are arranged in
A) an arrow
B) one circle
C) alternate positions
D) two circles
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: In a maize (monocot) leaf cross-section, the vascular bundles are typically arranged in two rows/circles (one closer to the upper surface, one closer to the lower), rather than the single ring seen in many dicot stems.

Q: Double fertilization is a unique feature of
A) angiosperms
B) bryophytes
C) pteridophytes
D) algae
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Double fertilization — where one sperm nucleus fuses with the egg to form the embryo, and a second sperm nucleus fuses with the polar nuclei to form the nutritive endosperm — is a defining reproductive feature unique to flowering plants (angiosperms).

Q: To facilitate gaseous exchange, breathing roots have
A) stomata
B) mitochondria
C) cuticle
D) lenticels
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Breathing roots (pneumatophores), such as those found in mangrove plants growing in waterlogged, low-oxygen soil, are studded with lenticels — small pores in their surface tissue — that allow atmospheric oxygen to diffuse in for the root's respiration.

Q: A wide pith with a ring of conducting tissue is characteristic of the root of
A) sunflower
B) maize
C) bean
D) okra
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: A wide central pith surrounded by a ring of vascular tissue is a typical feature of a monocot root, and maize is the classic monocot example among the options given (sunflower, bean, and okra are all dicots, whose roots instead have a small or absent pith with a star-shaped xylem core at the very centre).

Q: The flow of air and water in or out of the mesophyll layer of a leaf is controlled by the
A) stomata
B) lenticels
C) air spaces
D) guard cells
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: While the stoma is the actual pore, it is the pair of guard cells flanking it that physically control whether that pore is open or closed, thereby regulating the movement of gases and water vapour in and out of the leaf's internal air spaces.

Q: The major site of photosynthesis in the leaf is the
A) palisade parenchyma
B) mesophyll parenchyma
C) upper epidermis
D) lower epidermis
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: The palisade parenchyma (palisade mesophyll) consists of tightly packed, elongated cells directly beneath the upper epidermis, densely loaded with chloroplasts and positioned to receive maximum sunlight — making it the leaf's primary photosynthetic tissue.

Q: Production of naked seeds is a distinctive feature of the group of plants called
A) grasses
B) conifers
C) legumes
D) palms
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Conifers are gymnosperms, meaning their seeds are "naked" — not enclosed within a fruit or ovary wall, but exposed on the surface of cone scales — unlike grasses, legumes, and palms, which are all angiosperms whose seeds develop inside an enclosed fruit.

Q: Stomata pores open when there is
A) an increase in the sugar content of guard cells
B) a decrease in the osmotic concentration of guard cells
C) a decrease in the sugar content of mesophyll cells
D) an increase in the sugar content of mesophyll cells
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: In the presence of light, guard cells convert stored starch into sugar, which increases their internal solute (osmotic) concentration. Water then moves into the guard cells by osmosis, making them turgid and bowing outward, which opens the stomatal pore.

Q: Which of the following structures is capable of producing more tissues in the stem of a herbaceous flowering plant?
A) Epidermis
B) Pericycle
C) Xylem
D) Cambium
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The cambium is a meristematic tissue consisting of actively dividing cells capable of continuously generating new vascular tissue (xylem and phloem), unlike epidermis, mature xylem, or pericycle, which do not routinely produce new tissue in this way.

Q: Which of the following features are all associated with monocots?
A) Fibrous root system, branched network of veins, and one seed leaf
B) Fibrous root system, two seed leaves, and floral parts in threes
C) One seed leaf, petals in threes or groups of threes, and parallel venation of leaves
D) One seed leaf, net-veined leaves, and petals in three or multiples of three
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: A single seed leaf (cotyledon), floral parts in threes, and parallel leaf venation are all classic, consistent monocot features occurring together, whereas the other options mix in dicot traits (branched/net venation or two seed leaves) that don't belong with true monocot anatomy.

Q: Peripheral arrangement of vascular tissues in dicots is a characteristic of the
A) leaf
B) petiole
C) stem
D) root
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: In a dicot stem, the vascular bundles are typically arranged in a ring toward the periphery (outer region) around a central pith — a very different pattern from the central, star-shaped xylem core found in a dicot root.

Q: Most monocots are easily recognized by their
A) short leaves with petioles
B) long and sword-like leaves
C) long and palm-like leaves
D) short leaves with many veinlets
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Most monocot plants (such as grasses) have characteristically long, narrow, sword-shaped leaves with parallel veins and typically no distinct petiole, making this a useful field identification feature.

Q: Which of the following is true of the transverse section of a dicot stem?
A) The xylem is more interiorly located than the phloem
B) The cambium lies between the cortex and vascular bundles
C) The vascular bundles are randomly scattered within the cortex
D) The epidermis is completely encircled by the cortex
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: In each vascular bundle of a dicot stem, the xylem is positioned toward the inside (nearer the pith), with the phloem lying further out (toward the cortex) — making the xylem the more interiorly located tissue. Dicot vascular bundles are arranged in an organized ring, not randomly scattered, and it is the epidermis that surrounds the cortex, not the reverse.

Q: Seed plants are divided into
A) monocotyledons and dicotyledons
B) tracheophytes and bryophytes
C) ferns and mosses
D) angiosperms and gymnosperms
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: At the broadest level, seed plants (Spermatophyta) are divided into the two major groups angiosperms (flowering plants, with enclosed seeds) and gymnosperms (cone-bearing plants, with naked seeds). Monocots and dicots is a further subdivision specifically within angiosperms, not of seed plants as a whole.

Q: Plants that show secondary growth are usually found among the
A) thallophytes
B) pteridophytes
C) monocotyledons
D) dicotyledons
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: True secondary growth (increase in girth via a functional vascular cambium) is characteristic of dicotyledonous plants (and gymnosperms), while thallophytes, pteridophytes, and most monocots typically do not show this kind of woody secondary thickening.

Q: Which of the following plant structures lacks a waterproof cuticle?
A) Leaf
B) Stem
C) Root
D) Shoot
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Unlike the leaf, stem, and general shoot system (all of which are covered by a waterproof cuticle to prevent excess water loss), the root's absorbing surface (epidermis/root hairs) lacks this cuticle, since a cuticle would block the very water absorption the root needs to perform.

Q: Which of the following is involved in secondary thickening in plants?
A) Collenchyma and xylem cells
B) Vascular cambium and cambium arch
C) Vascular cambium and cork cambium
D) Vascular cambium and collenchyma cells
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Secondary thickening in woody dicot stems is driven jointly by two lateral meristems: the vascular cambium, which produces new wood (secondary xylem) and increases the stem's girth internally, and the cork cambium, which produces the protective bark (cork) on the outside.

Q: Seed plants are the most dominant vegetation on land because of
A) their motile gametes
B) their ability to photosynthesize
C) efficient seed dispersal
D) availability of water
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Seed plants owe much of their dominance on land to their efficient means of seed dispersal (by wind, animals, water, and other mechanisms), allowing them to colonize new areas widely and successfully — unlike more primitive plants, which remain tied to water for reproduction due to their motile gametes.
