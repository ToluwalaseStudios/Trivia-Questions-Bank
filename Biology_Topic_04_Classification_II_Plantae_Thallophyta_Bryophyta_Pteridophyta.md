# TOPIC 4: CLASSIFICATION II — PLANTAE: THALLOPHYTA, BRYOPHYTA, PTERIDOPHYTA
(JAMB Past Questions)

Q: The following events take place during conjugation in Spirogyra: (1) The protoplasm in each of the conjugating cells separates from the cell wall to form a gamete; the gamete of one filament passes through the conjugation tube to meet the gamete of the other filament (2) the zygote soon becomes surrounded by a thick brown wall to become a zygospore, which bursts after a short rest and germinates to produce a new filament (3) the nuclei of the gametes fuse to form a zygote (4) the protuberances meet and where they touch, the cell wall disappears so that a conjugation tube joining the two cells is formed (5) protuberances appear on the walls of cells of two filaments of Spirogyra lying side by side opposite one another. What is the correct sequence in which the above events occur?
A) 1,2,3,4,5
B) 1,2,4,5,3
C) 3,4,5,2,1
D) 5,4,1,3,2
E) 5,4,3,1,2
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Conjugation logically must begin with two filaments lying side by side developing outward bulges (5 — protuberances appear), which then meet and fuse into a connecting bridge (4 — the conjugation tube forms). Only then can the cell contents round up into gametes and one gamete migrate across the tube to the other cell (1). Once united, the two gamete nuclei fuse to form a zygote (3), which finally develops a thick protective wall to become the resting zygospore (2). This gives the order 5, 4, 1, 3, 2.

Q: One of the following statements is Not true of Spirogyra
A) It is a simple multicellular plant
B) During conjugation, its gametes, which are structurally and physiologically similar, fuse to form a zygote
C) It possesses spiral chloroplast which enable the plant to photosynthesize
D) Its cells are protected externally by a layer of mucilage
E) There are pyrenoids along the chloroplast
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Although the gametes of Spirogyra look structurally identical (isogametes), they are not physiologically identical — one filament's gametes behave as the "male" (motile, moving through the conjugation tube) and the other's as the "female" (stays put, receives the other gamete), even though nothing about their shape or size distinguishes them. Calling them "physiologically similar" is therefore inaccurate, making B the false statement. Spirogyra genuinely is multicellular, has spiral ribbon-like chloroplasts with pyrenoids, and is coated in a slimy mucilage layer.

Q: One common feature of the fungi, algae, mosses and ferns is that they
A) are photosynthetic
B) show alternation of generation
C) reproduce by means of conjugation
D) can survive dry conditions
E) have no seeds
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: None of these four groups (fungi, algae, mosses, ferns) produce true seeds — seed production is a feature of the more advanced spermatophytes (gymnosperms and angiosperms) only. Fungi are not photosynthetic, conjugation is specific to certain algae/fungi (not universal to all four), and alternation of generations, while present in algae/mosses/ferns, is not a defining feature of fungi.

Q: Which of the following represents the evolutionary sequence in these plants? (1) Flowering plants (2) Ferns (3) Mosses (4) Algae (5) Conifers
A) 2→1→4→3→5
B) 5→4→3→2→1
C) 2→4→5→1→3
D) 3→2→4→5→1
E) 4→3→2→5→1
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: Plant evolution runs from the simplest to the most advanced groups: algae (4) evolved first, followed by mosses (3), then ferns (2), then the seed-bearing conifers/gymnosperms (5), and finally the most advanced group, the flowering plants/angiosperms (1) — giving the sequence 4→3→2→5→1.

Q: Mosses, liverworts and ferns can be grouped together because they
A) are all aquatic plants
B) all grow in deserts
C) are seedless plants
D) have undifferentiated plant bodies
E) all produce colourless flowers
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Mosses, liverworts, and ferns all reproduce by spores rather than seeds, making "seedless plants" the shared feature that groups them together. They are not exclusively aquatic or desert plants, and ferns (unlike mosses/liverworts) do have differentiated roots, stems, and leaves — and none of them produce flowers at all.

Q: Spirogyra and Mucor can be grouped together as Thallophyta because
A) they are unicellular organisms
B) their spores could be dispersed by wind
C) they are capable of living independent lives
D) they reproduce sexually only
E) their bodies are made up of thallus and filaments alternatively
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: The defining feature of the group Thallophyta is that the plant body is a simple, undifferentiated "thallus" — which in the case of these particular organisms takes the form of simple filaments (Spirogyra's chain of cells, or Mucor's hyphal threads) rather than true roots, stems, and leaves. Neither organism is unicellular (both are filamentous/multicellular), and both can reproduce asexually as well as sexually.

Q: Which of the following is INCORRECT? The prothallus of a fern
A) is a flattened heart-shaped structure
B) is green because its cells contain chloroplasts
C) is the dominant plant
D) bears the sexual organs
E) is attached to the ground by numerous rhizoids
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: In ferns, it is the sporophyte (the familiar large leafy fern plant) that is the dominant, conspicuous generation — not the prothallus, which is a small, inconspicuous, short-lived gametophyte stage. The prothallus is indeed heart-shaped, green (photosynthetic), bears the sexual organs (antheridia and archegonia), and is anchored by rhizoids.

Q: In Spirogyra, the Pyrenoid
A) excretes waste products
B) is suspended by cytoplasmic strands
C) is mainly used for respiration
D) usually contains starch
E) makes the plant slimy to touch
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Pyrenoids are small protein-rich bodies attached to the spiral chloroplast of Spirogyra, and they function as centres where starch is synthesized and accumulated from the products of photosynthesis. (It is the nucleus, not the pyrenoid, that is suspended by cytoplasmic strands in the cell.)

Q: Bryophytes are different from flowering plants because they
A) live in most habitats
B) are small plants
C) reproduce sexually and asexually
D) have small leaves
E) have no vascular tissues
ANSWER: E
EXPLANATION: The single most fundamental difference is that bryophytes (mosses and liverworts) lack true vascular (conducting) tissues — xylem and phloem — which flowering plants possess. This absence of vascular tissue is exactly why bryophytes remain small and restricted to moist habitats, but the vascular tissue distinction itself is the defining, non-negotiable difference.

Q: In lower plants like mosses, the structure which performs the functions of roots of higher plants is called
A) root hairs
B) rootlets
C) hyphae
D) rhizoids
E) thalli
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Mosses have no true roots; instead, they anchor themselves to the substrate and absorb some water using thread-like structures called rhizoids, which perform a root-like anchoring function without possessing any true root tissue.

Q: Which of the following is NOT true of Spirogyra?
A) Reproduces by conjugation
B) Reproduces by fragmentation
C) Consists of branched filaments
D) Consists of unbranched filaments
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Spirogyra filaments are unbranched — they consist of a single chain of cylindrical cells joined end to end, never dividing into branches. Spirogyra does reproduce both sexually (by conjugation) and asexually (by fragmentation, where a broken piece of filament grows into a new filament).

Q: In a plant exhibiting alternation of generations, the diploid multicellular stage is known as
A) gametophyte
B) spermatophyte
C) holophyte
D) sporophyte
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: In the alternation-of-generations life cycle, the sporophyte is the diploid (2n) multicellular stage that produces spores by meiosis. The gametophyte, by contrast, is the haploid (n) stage that produces gametes.

Q: A characteristic that distinguishes bryophytes from flowering plants is the
A) possession of true stems and leaves
B) ability to reproduce asexually
C) absence of vascular tissues
D) ability to grow in moist habitats
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: As with an earlier question in this set, the absence of vascular (conducting) tissue in bryophytes is the key structural distinction that separates them from flowering plants, which possess well-developed xylem and phloem.

Q: In most true ferns, sporangia are grouped into
A) indusium
B) fronds
C) prothalli
D) sori
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Clusters of sporangia on the underside of a fern frond are grouped into structures called sori (singular: sorus). The indusium is simply the protective flap covering a sorus, the frond is the fern's leaf, and the prothallus is the separate gametophyte generation.

Q: In the reproduction of mosses, water is essential because
A) they live in moist habitats
B) they cannot reproduce without water
C) the male gametes must swim to fertilize the ovum
D) they produce spores
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Mosses have flagellated, swimming male gametes (antherozoids) that require a film of water to physically swim from the antheridium to the archegonium in order to fertilize the egg cell (ovum) — this is the specific reason water is essential for their sexual reproduction.

Q: The term 'Thallophyta' refers to
A) ferns and mosses
B) algae and fungi
C) mosses and liverworts
D) fungi and ferns
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Thallophyta is the traditional grouping for plant-like organisms whose bodies are a simple, undifferentiated thallus rather than true roots/stems/leaves — this classically includes the algae and the fungi.

Q: The prothallus of a fern is equivalent to the gametophyte generation of a moss because it
A) is inconspicuous
B) has rhizoids
C) bears sexual organs
D) is multicellular
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Both the fern prothallus and the moss's leafy gametophyte plant are the haploid generation responsible for producing the sexual organs (antheridia and archegonia) and the gametes — this shared reproductive role is what makes them functionally equivalent generations, even though the fern prothallus is comparatively far less conspicuous than the moss gametophyte.

Q: The algae, bryophytes and pteridophytes are similar in that they
A) are sea weeds
B) have no vascular tissues
C) require moisture for fertilization
D) are microscopic plants
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: All three groups produce motile, swimming male gametes that need a film of external water to reach and fertilize the female gamete — so moisture is essential for fertilization across algae, bryophytes, and pteridophytes alike. (Note that pteridophytes, unlike bryophytes, do have vascular tissue, so B cannot be the shared feature.)

Q: The spores of ferns are dispersed by
A) wind
B) water
C) insects
D) explosive mechanism
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The sporangium wall of a fern includes a specialized ring of cells called the annulus, which builds up tension as it dries and eventually snaps back suddenly, catapulting the spores explosively away from the parent plant.

Q: In bryophytes, sex organs are produced in the
A) gametophyte
B) rhizoid
C) protonema
D) sporophyte
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: The gametophyte generation (the familiar leafy moss plant or liverwort thallus) is the haploid stage responsible for bearing the sex organs — antheridia (male) and archegonia (female) — and producing the gametes.

Q: Which of the following are differentiated into true roots, stems and leaves?
A) Algae
B) Schizophyta
C) Pteridophyta
D) Bryophyta
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Pteridophytes (ferns and their relatives) are the simplest plant group to possess true vascular tissue, and with it, genuinely differentiated roots, stems, and leaves — unlike algae, schizophytes (bacteria/blue-green algae), and bryophytes, all of which lack true vascular structures.

Q: The annulus of fern sporangium helps in
A) spore dispersal
B) conduction of mineral salt
C) trapping of light energy
D) water retention
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: As explained earlier, the annulus is a ring of specialized thick-walled cells on the sporangium that dries out, builds tension, and then snaps to catapult the spores away — its role is entirely about aiding spore dispersal.

Q: Which of the following correctly summarizes the life cycle of a fern plant?
A) Spore→prothallus→thallus sporangium
B) Male and Female→gametangia→zygospore→sporangium→spores
C) Spore→thallus→spermatozoa→ovum→sporangium
D) Prothallus→spermatozoid→egg cell→leafy plant→sporangiospore
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: A fern's life cycle correctly runs: the prothallus (gametophyte) produces a spermatozoid (male gamete) that swims to fertilize the egg cell (female gamete) in the archegonium; this fertilized egg then develops into the mature leafy fern plant (sporophyte), which eventually produces spores in its sporangia — matching the sequence given in option D.

Q: In which of these features do bryophytes differ from pteridophytes?
A) Absence of flower
B) Alternation of generation
C) Dependence on water for reproduction
D) Presence of a vascular system
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Pteridophytes (ferns) possess true vascular tissue (xylem and phloem), while bryophytes (mosses/liverworts) do not — this is the key structural difference between the two groups. Both groups lack flowers, both show alternation of generations, and both require water for the swimming male gametes to reach the egg.

Q: Which of the following organs or cell components are common to both the sporophyte and the gametophyte of a fern?
A) Rhizoids
B) Roots
C) Chloroplasts
D) Leaves
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Both the fern's sporophyte (the leafy plant) and its gametophyte (the prothallus) are photosynthetic and therefore contain chloroplasts in their cells. However, only the sporophyte has true roots and leaves, while only the prothallus has rhizoids (root-like anchoring structures, not true roots).

Q: Which is the correct order in an evolutionary sequence for the following plant groups?
A) Bacteria→ferns→algae→mosses→seed plant
B) Bacteria→ferns→mosses→algae→seed plants
C) Bacteria→algae→mosses→ferns→seed plant
D) Bacteria mosses algae ferns seed plants
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: The evolutionary order of increasing complexity runs from the simplest prokaryotes (bacteria) through algae, then mosses, then ferns, and finally to the seed-bearing plants — matching option C exactly.

Q: One common characteristic of fungi, algae, mosses and ferns is that they
A) show alternation of generations
B) reproduce sexually by conjugation
C) produce spores that are dispersed
D) possess chlorophyll in their tissues
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: All four groups reproduce using spores that are released and dispersed to form new individuals. Fungi lack chlorophyll (ruling out D), conjugation as a reproductive method is specific to certain algae and fungi rather than universal (ruling out B), and while alternation of generations occurs in algae/mosses/ferns, it is not a defining feature of fungi (ruling out A).

Q: In ferns, the sporophyte
A) develops from a haploid zygote
B) reproduces sexually to produce spores
C) is haploid and dependent on the gametophyte
D) is diploid and independent of the gametophyte
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: The fern sporophyte (the familiar leafy fern plant) develops from a diploid zygote, grows independently once established, and reproduces asexually by producing haploid spores (not sexually) — making D the accurate description.

Q: In which of the following organisms does each cell combine the function of nutrition, reproduction and growth?
A) Rhizopus
B) Dryopteris
C) Brachymenium
D) Spirogyra
ANSWER: D
EXPLANATION: Although Spirogyra is a filamentous (chain-like) alga, each individual cell within the filament is functionally independent and capable of carrying out nutrition, growth, and reproduction on its own (for example, any single cell can participate in conjugation or contribute to fragmentation), unlike the more differentiated, multicellular bodies of Rhizopus (a fungus), Dryopteris (a fern), or Brachymenium (a moss).

Q: One distinctive feature in the life history of liverworts is that they exhibit
A) Alternation of generation
B) Vegetative reproduction
C) Asexual reproduction
D) Sexual reproduction
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Like mosses and ferns, liverworts show a true alternation of generations between a haploid gametophyte stage and a diploid sporophyte stage — this is the most distinctive and defining feature of their life history, since asexual/vegetative/sexual reproduction individually are not unique to liverworts alone.

Q: The absence of special food and water-conducting systems restricts the body size in
A) algae, liverworts and mosses
B) liverworts, mosses and ferns
C) the bryophytes and the pteridophytes
D) the thallophytes and the pteridophytes
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Algae, liverworts, and mosses all lack true vascular (conducting) tissue for transporting water and food efficiently over long distances, which limits how large their bodies can grow. Ferns (pteridophytes), by contrast, do possess vascular tissue and can therefore grow considerably larger.

Q: The dominant phase in the life cycle of a bryophyte is the
A) gametangium
B) sporophyte
C) gametophyte
D) prothallus
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: In bryophytes (mosses and liverworts), the gametophyte is the larger, longer-lived, and more conspicuous generation — the familiar green, leafy moss plant is itself the gametophyte, while the sporophyte remains small and dependent on it.

Q: A water medium is necessary for fertilization in
A) fungi
B) conifers
C) ferns
D) angiosperms
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Ferns retain motile, swimming male gametes that require a film of water to reach the egg cell for fertilization, just as in mosses. Conifers and angiosperms, more evolutionarily advanced, use pollen (carried by wind or animals) rather than swimming gametes, so external water is no longer needed for their fertilization.

Q: Alternation of asexual and sexual modes of reproduction is found in
A) blue-green algae
B) Euglena
C) fern
D) maize
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: Ferns show a true alternation of generations, in which the sexually reproducing gametophyte (prothallus) generation alternates with the asexually spore-producing sporophyte (leafy fern) generation. Blue-green algae and Euglena reproduce mainly asexually, and maize (a flowering plant) reproduces only sexually via seeds, without a comparable alternating asexual spore-producing stage.

Q: The dominant phase in the life cycle of a fern is the
A) prothallus
B) sporophyte
C) antheridium
D) gametophyte
ANSWER: B
EXPLANATION: Unlike in bryophytes, in ferns it is the sporophyte (the large, leafy fern plant) that is the dominant, long-lived generation, while the gametophyte (prothallus) is small, short-lived, and much less conspicuous.

Q: In bryophytes, sex organs are produced in the
A) protonema
B) sporophyte
C) gametophyte
D) rhizoid
ANSWER: C
EXPLANATION: As established earlier, the gametophyte generation of a bryophyte bears the antheridia and archegonia (sex organs) responsible for producing the male and female gametes.

Q: Alternation of generation is a feature shown in
A) mosses
B) fungi
C) grasses
D) conifers
ANSWER: A
EXPLANATION: Mosses (bryophytes) show a clear, textbook alternation of generations between a dominant haploid gametophyte and a dependent diploid sporophyte. Fungi, grasses, and conifers do not follow this same alternating pattern of two distinct, free-living generations in the same way.
