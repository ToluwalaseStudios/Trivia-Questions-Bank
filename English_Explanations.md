## SECTION A: Comprehension (Political education passage), Q1–Q5

**Q1. Nearest opposite of "defile" as used in the passage**
**Answer: C, sanctify** | ✔
The passage says education was being "defiled by politics", meaning polluted or made impure. The opposite is to sanctify, which means to make holy or pure. Forgery is faking, degenerate means to decline, and disparage means to belittle.

**Q2. Main purpose of political education according to the passage**
**Answer: C, To create an egalitarian society** | You picked D ✘
The first paragraph defines it as preparing citizens to act towards socio-economic objectives: abolishing poverty and creating a modern, democratic, secular, socialist society "in place of the... hierarchical and inegalitarian one". That is an egalitarian society. Option D (high intellectual acumen) is never mentioned. Option A (elitism) is what the writer criticises. Option B concerns reform of the education system, not the purpose of political education.

**Q3. Relationship between politics and educational institutions after independence**
**Answer: D, There was substantial interference without political support** | You picked C ✘
The passage says academicians wanted "political support without political interference", but "what we have actually received is infinite political interference with little genuine political support". So there was heavy interference and hardly any real support. Option C wrongly says they got support at the cost of interference.

**Q4. Most opposite in meaning to "hamper"**
**Answer: C, foster** | ✔
Hamper means to hinder or obstruct ("hamper radical social transformation"). Foster means to encourage and promote. Accelerate means to speed up, which is about pace, not about helping or obstructing. Envision and initiate do not carry that meaning.

**Q5. Major drawback of the present education system**
**Answer: A, The present education system promotes the domination of the privileged few** | You picked C ✘
The last paragraph says the system "continues to support domination of the privileged groups and domestication of the under-privileged ones". Option C is self-contradictory: the passage calls the system hierarchical and *in*egalitarian, but option C says "egalitarian". Option B (British model) is not given as a drawback. Option D says the opposite of the passage.

## SECTION B: Vocabulary (nearest in meaning), Q6–Q10

**Q6. "a serious REVERSE"**
**Answer: C, a defeat** | ✔
"Triumphant rebels... met with a serious reverse" means a setback or defeat after winning. The contrast with "triumphant" confirms this.

**Q7. PETTIFOGGER**
**Answer: A, A low-class lawyer given to mean dealings** | ✔
A pettifogger is a petty, unscrupulous lawyer who uses trivial, tricky or dishonest practices. Poverty tempting a lawyer to such dealings fits the sentence.

**Q8. "TRIPPED UP halfway through the race"**
**Answer: A, interrupted** | ✔
To trip someone up is to make them stumble, which stops their progress. The sentence says that if Joy had not been stopped midway she would have won. "Interrupted" captures that. She was not necessarily exhausted, discouraged or disqualified.

**Q9. APPOSITE**
**Answer: C, appropriate** | You picked D ✘
Apposite means highly suitable or relevant to the matter. "Inconsequential" means unimportant, which is nearly the opposite of apposite.

**Q10. PRESAGE**
**Answer: C, Sign** | ✔
A presage is an omen or warning sign that something is about to happen. "Was there any presage before the incident?" means "any sign".

## SECTION C: Fill the gaps, Q11–Q15

**Q11. "The attitude of my students to ___ baffles me"**
**Answer: B, Pronunciation** | ✔
The noun is spelt pro-nun-ci-ation (no "ou", unlike the verb pronounce).

**Q12. "Dr Josh Unusual saw Dr. Jeremiah ___ an egg on the wall"**
**Answer: A, Smash** | ✔
After a verb of perception (see, watch, hear) plus an object, use the bare infinitive for a complete action. "Saw him smash an egg" shows the whole short action.

**Q13. "Toluwani ___ Dr. Jeremiah's salient points since she came home"**
**Answer: C, Has been reading** | You picked A ✘
"Since + a point in the past (simple past)" needs the present perfect, and the continuous form shows the action has gone on up to now: **has been reading**. "Had been reading" is past perfect continuous and needs another past event to anchor it. "Has read" does not show the ongoing activity.

**Q14. "Amina is an ___ of the University"**
**Answer: D, Alumna** | You picked B ✘
Amina is a female name, so the singular female form alumna is needed. Alumnus is male singular, alumni is plural (or mixed group), and alumnae is female plural.

**Q15. "Your performance will improve if you ___ hard"**
**Answer: D, Practise** | ✔
This is a first conditional: "will + verb" in the main clause and the simple present in the if-clause. Practise (with s) is the verb; practice is the noun.

## SECTION D: Most appropriate completion, Q16–Q25

**Q16. "The Lagos Trade Fair ___ annually"**
**Answer: D, is held** | ✔
A fair is held (passive), and "annually" shows a habitual action, so the simple present passive is used. The subject is singular, so "is".

**Q17. "Sola ran ___"**
**Answer: C, round the town twice before supper** | ✔
Standard order is place first, then frequency, then time: "round the town" + "twice" + "before supper". Option B wrongly adds "the" before supper, and A and D break the word order.

**Q18. "He promised to do the work in no time."**
**Answer: E, very quickly** | ✔
"In no time" is an idiom meaning very fast or almost immediately.

**Q19. "John's father has just bought a ___ car"**
**Answer: C, brand new blue Japanese sports** | ✔
Adjective order: opinion, size, age (brand new), shape, colour (blue), origin (Japanese), material, purpose or type (sports): brand new blue Japanese sports car.

**Q20. "The delegates were given ___"**
**Answer: B, A two-hour break** | ✔
A number + unit used as an adjective before a noun is hyphenated, and the unit stays singular: a two-hour break.

**Q21. "Garba acquiesced ___ their decision"**
**Answer: A, In** | You picked C ✘
The verb takes the preposition "in" (acquiesce in = accept without protest).

**Q22. "Snakes and ladders always ___ me happy these days"**
**Answer: B, Makes** | You picked C ✘
"Snakes and Ladders" is the name of one game, so it takes a singular verb: always makes. "Always... these days" calls for the simple present.

**Q23. "The property of the jailed former local government chairman have been ___"**
**Answer: B, Confiscated** | You picked A ✘
This tests spelling: confiscated (c-o-n-f-i-s-c-a-t-e-d). The other options are misspelt. (Strictly the verb should be "has been", because property is singular, but that is not what is being tested.)

**Q24. "I will give you the money next week ___"**
**Answer: C, Without fail** | You picked D ✘
"Without fail" is the fixed idiom meaning for certain, no matter what. The other forms are not idiomatic.

**Q25. "The greatness of a country is believed to ___ the well-being of its citizens"**
**Answer: D, Consist in** | You picked A ✘
"Consist in" means to lie in or have as its essential nature. "Consist of" means to be made up of. "Comprise" takes no preposition, and "comprise of" is generally regarded as incorrect.

## SECTION E: Most nearly OPPOSITE in meaning, Q26–Q30

**Q26. COMPROMISING (a Senate President too accommodating to the executive)**
**Answer: C, rigid** | You picked B ✘
Compromising means ready to give way and make concessions. The opposite is firm and unyielding: rigid. Knowledgeable and resourceful are unrelated. Intransigent (A) is also close in meaning, but the keyed answer is rigid.

**Q27. ALLAYED (fears)**
**Answer: A, Aggravated** | You picked D ✘
To allay fears is to calm or relieve them. The opposite is to make them worse: aggravated. Assuaged and mitigated are synonyms of allayed.

**Q28. OPINIONATED**
**Answer: D, an objective** | ✔
An opinionated person holds personal views stubbornly. The opposite is someone who judges impartially, on facts: objective. Subjective is close in meaning to opinionated. Discursive means rambling.

**Q29. WARY OF (night journeys)**
**Answer: C, satisfied with** | You picked D ✘
Wary means cautious, apprehensive, suspicious. Troubled about and worried about are synonyms, so they are out. The opposite is being comfortable with, or satisfied with. "Unscared of" is also close in sense, but it is not standard English; the keyed answer is C.

**Q30. SANGUINE (the boy) ... the girl was ___**
**Answer: B, Hopeless** | You picked D ✘
Sanguine means optimistic and hopeful. The girl is contrasted with the boy, so she must be the opposite: hopeless. Hopeful is a synonym, and "unserious" is not the opposite of hopeful.

## SECTION F: Gap-fill passage on English spelling (gaps 31–40), Q31–Q40
*Each question number matches its gap number in the passage. Option letters follow the order printed in the passage.*

**Q31. Gap (31): "There is often ___ between the pronunciation of a word and its spelling"**
**Answer: A, discrepancy**
A discrepancy is a mismatch, which is the writer's point: spelling often does not match the sound. "A similarity" and "concord" say the opposite of what the passage argues, and "discord" means disagreement between people.

**Q32. Gap (32): "One cannot always tell how to spell a word ___ its pronunciation"**
**Answer: B, by**
"By its pronunciation" means using the sound as a guide, which is the idea of the sentence. "As from" and "as to" are not natural here.

**Q33. Gap (33): "the peculiarity of English spelling ___"**
**Answer: C, system**
The passage opens with "its spelling system" and later says "there was no system for spelling its words". So the phrase is "English spelling system".

**Q34. Gap (34): "While the ___ people spoke English, the literate upper classes spoke French and wrote in Latin"**
**Answer: C, less enlightened**
The sentence contrasts English speakers with the "literate upper classes", and the previous sentence says they "could neither read nor write". "Less enlightened" fits that group. "Middle-class" does not, because the contrast is with the upper classes, not a middle class, and "educated" contradicts "could neither read nor write". "Uneducated" is close; the keyed answer is "less enlightened".

**Q35. Gap (35): "later when English became a ___ language"**
**Answer: C, written**
The passage is about English moving from speech to being put on paper. "Written language" fits. "Literary" would suggest a language of literature, which is not the point here.

**Q36. Gap (36): "the first writers of English were French-speaking ___"**
**Answer: B, scribes**
Scribes are professional writers or copyists of documents. The sentence is about the first people who wrote English down, not speakers (orators), newscasters or interpreters.

**Q37. Gap (37): "they carried many French spelling ___ into English"**
**Answer: C, idiosyncrasies**
Idiosyncrasies are peculiar individual features. It fits the French scribes' particular ways of spelling.

**Q38. Gap (38): "these first writers... often ___ letters into words even when they were not pronounced"**
**Answer: A, inserted**
"Inserted" is the plain, accurate verb for putting extra letters into words. "Interjected" means to say something abruptly, "interpolated" refers to adding material into a text (usually something added later), and "juxtaposed" means placed side by side.

**Q39. Gap (39): "because the ___ word in Latin was spelled that way"**
**Answer: C, corresponding**
"Corresponding" means matching: the Latin word that matched the English one. "Antecedent" means something that came before, "opposing" and "synonymous" do not fit the sense.

**Q40. Gap (40): "the ___ of certain words changed while the spelling remained the same"**
**Answer: D, pronunciation**
The point of the whole passage is the gap between how words are said and how they are spelt. If the pronunciation changed while the spelling stayed, the two drift apart.

## SECTION G: Idioms and expressions, Q41–Q50

**Q41. "The house is going under the hammer."**

**Q42. "A Gilbertian situation"**
**Answer: B, Humorous situation** | You picked A ✘
It comes from W. S. Gilbert, who wrote comic operas full of absurd, topsy-turvy situations. A Gilbertian situation is humorously absurd.

**Q43. "FLEA MARKETS"**

A flea market sells used and bargain goods.

**Q44. "exude SHENANIGANS"**

Shenanigans are mischief, trickery and deceitful behaviour. The context (politicians) points to dishonesty.

**Q45. "a BIT OF CRUMPET"**

In British slang, crumpet means an attractive person. "A bit of crumpet" says she is good-looking.

**Q46. "a dead ringer for Bagudu"**
**Answer: C, Looks like Bagudu in appearance** | ✔
A dead ringer is someone who looks exactly like another person.

**Q47. "The woman powders her nose every evening"**

"Powder one's nose" is a polite euphemism for going to the toilet or restroom.

**Q48. "OFF THE RECORD"**

Said off the record means it is unofficial and must not be quoted or published.

**Q49. "steal the show"**

To steal the show is to draw the most attention away from others. "Collective idea" means it was a joint idea.

**Q50. "injured by a friendly fire"**

Friendly fire is fire from one's own forces that hits their own side. Options A and B wrongly make it about one personal friend.

## SECTION H: Oral English, Q51–Q60

**Q51. Vowel sound in S*ay*s**
**Answer: D, Said** | ✔
"Says" is pronounced /sez/, with the vowel /e/. "Said" is /sed/, with the same vowel. Day, days and pays have /eɪ/.

**Q52. Vowel sound in C*our*se**
**Answer: A, Coarse** | You picked D ✘
Course is /kɔːs/, and coarse is /kɔːs/ (a homophone), so they have the same vowel /ɔː/. Worse is /wɜːs/, curse is /kɜːs/, and cot is /kɒt/, which has a short vowel.

**Q53. Favour*I*te**
**Answer: C, Kin** | ✔
The marked letter in favourite is I, which is pronounced /ɪ/, as in kin /kɪn/. Fight and write have /aɪ/, and keen has /iː/. (The instruction says "consonant", but the item being matched is the sound of the marked letter I.)

**Q54. The consonant sound /l/**
**Answer: A, Table** | ✔
The /l/ is pronounced in "table" (/ˈteɪbl/). In calm, would and talk the "l" is silent.

**Q55. Rhymes with "coax" /kəʊks/**
**Answer: A, Chokes** | ✔
Chokes /tʃəʊks/ ends in the same sound /əʊks/. Rose ends in /z/, lose has /uː/, coarse is /kɔːs/.

**Q56. Rhymes with "brother" /ˈbrʌðə/**
**Answer: A, Mother** | ✔
Mother /ˈmʌðə/ has the same ending /ʌðə/. Father has /ɑːðə/, broader has /ɔːdə/ and brothel has /ɒθl/.

**Q57. Stress in "egregious"**
**Answer: A, eGREgious** | ✔
It is /ɪˈɡriːdʒəs/, with the stress on the second syllable: e-GRE-gious.

**Q58. Stress in "cooperative"**
**Answer: C, coOperative** | You picked D ✘
It is /kəʊˈɒpərətɪv/, with the main stress on the second syllable: co-OP-er-a-tive. The stress does not move to "-ra-".

**Q59. "My father READ the newspaper" (stress on READ)**
**Answer: B, Did my father steal the newspaper?** | ✔
The emphasis on READ contrasts it with a different action. The reply sets read against steal: no, he *read* it (he didn't steal it).

**Q60. "The president SPOKE to the press" (stress on SPOKE)**
**Answer: B, Did the president write to the press?** | ✔
The stress on SPOKE contrasts it with another way of communicating, here writing: he *spoke* to them, he didn't write.
